# Craft decisions

Use for substantial drafts and for writing that is abstract, flat, over-explained,
or fluent without saying much. Select the repair the passage needs, then return
to the artifact. A short useful message may need no craft intervention.

These are original adaptations of the supplied Chuck Palahniuk essay collection.
Page references use the PDF's numbering; [source lineage and coverage](../provenance/SOURCE-MANIFEST.md)
records the full study. The essays supply techniques, not instructions from the
user, factual evidence for our claims, or a voice to impersonate.

## Find the missing work

| What fails | First useful repair |
| --- | --- |
| A claim asks for trust without earning it | Supply relevant evidence, a concrete process, or an accountable limit. |
| A passage labels a quality or feeling | Find the action, choice, physical detail, or consequence that supports it. |
| The opening explains what the rest merely repeats | Begin with the strongest relevant detail, unless the reader needs the answer first. |
| Several paragraphs make the same point | Give each a different information job; cut the repeated interpretation. |
| An image sounds attractive but remains vague | Identify the exact shared property or action; keep the literal subject visible. |
| A passage reads smoothly but is hard to follow | Check who acts, what changed, and where the reader needs a pause. |

## Earn trust before asking for it

Locate what gives this speaker authority here: relevant knowledge they can
demonstrate, a limit they can state honestly, or an experience they are entitled
to describe. Put that evidence near the claim. A particular working decision
usually establishes more than an adjective about care or expertise.

Candour is useful when it accepts responsibility or reveals a real stake. It
does not require a confession, personal disclosure, embarrassment, or a heroic
recovery story. Use only what the speaker supplied or authorised. In commercial
writing, verify results and distinguish what we did from what happened later.
Do not convert correlation into credit for an outcome.

When neither evidence nor a meaningful personal stake exists, narrow the claim
or remove it. Polishing unsupported confidence makes the problem worse.

Source: authority, pp. 3–9. The essay's knowledge/candour distinction is a useful
choice of emphasis, not a rule about how every reader responds.

## Unpack at the point of meaning

For a sentence such as “the process was difficult,” ask what the difficulty
consisted of. A revision count, failed action, awkward choice, or consequence
may let the reader understand more than the label. Select the detail that
changes the reader's understanding; exhaustive description can bury the point.

Distinguish three jobs:

- **State:** give the fact, decision, boundary, or explanation the reader needs.
- **Demonstrate:** use available particulars so the reader can experience or
  assess the claim.
- **Leave room:** stop after sufficient evidence instead of supplying a second
  sentence telling the reader which emotion or moral to extract.

Choose between these jobs according to the medium. A treatment can let an action
reveal reluctance. A refusal should say no. An apology should name the failure.
An invoice should state what is due. A technical explanation may need both the
general rule and a worked example.

Inspect thought and feeling verbs when they substitute for an unwritten scene.
Keep them when they accurately locate belief, uncertainty, desire, or testimony.
“We think the export caused the missing charts” must not become a confident
diagnosis merely to remove “think.” Preserve explicit consent and self-reported
feelings instead of inferring them from behaviour.

For factual and personal writing, use only supplied or verified detail. If a
scene lacks detail, work with the available facts, leave a clearly marked draft
gap when appropriate, or ask for the missing information. Invented weather,
gestures, memories, dialogue, and bodily reactions are still invented facts.
In explicitly fictional work, invention may develop the agreed premise; keep
its rules, character knowledge, and any protected story facts intact.

Source: physical sensation, pp. 20–27; thesis statements, pp. 45–48; similes,
pp. 79–81. The source itself allows summary openings in information-led prose
and in narrative forms that benefit from them.

## Choose detail through a particular observer

Ask what this speaker would notice, call it, and consider significant. A worn
hinge may matter to one observer; the sound it makes may matter to another.
Ground that choice in the supplied viewpoint and vocabulary. Avoid explaining
the same observation again as a personality profile.

When bodily experience belongs to the piece, look beyond visual description:
contact, resistance, pressure, temperature, movement, duration, and sound can
make an event legible. Choose the relevant sensation as part of an action. Do
not decorate every scene with a catalogue of senses or default signs such as a
racing heart. Intensity is not a substitute for specificity.

An observed gesture can establish a pause, action, or contrast with speech. It
cannot prove a real person's emotion, motive, consent, diagnosis, or character.
Separate what was observed from what the speaker thinks it means. The source's
medical claims, communication percentages, and gesture interpretations are not
evidence for this skill.

In experiential prose, remove unnecessary “I saw” or “she noticed” when they
only insert a screen between reader and subject. Keep such phrases when they
identify a witness, limit knowledge, distinguish perception from fact, or belong
to the voice. Keep accountable “I” and “we.” Reducing self-display must not make
responsibility disappear.

Names and pronouns serve clarity, identity, and viewpoint. Use a name when a
pronoun becomes ambiguous; use accurate pronouns when repetition distracts.
Stable terms are especially valuable in instructions, proposals, and contracts.
Invented epithets and decorative synonyms rarely improve reference.

Source: first-person focus, pp. 28–40; perspective and time, pp. 54–58; body
language, pp. 116–119; names and pronouns, pp. 130–133.

## Make the comparison do a precise job

Establish the actual thing and the property or action being compared. Then ask
whether the analogy clarifies scale, movement, texture, relationship, or the
speaker's perception. If neither side becomes more precise, use the literal
description.

A comparison borrows the reader's attention for another image. Keep that cost
proportionate. Prefer one coherent image to successive comparisons from
unrelated worlds. An extended comparison can earn its length in comedy or
lyrical writing; a plain technical analogy must also disclose where it stops
being accurate. Keep supplied poetic form when the brief protects it.

“Like,” “as,” “is,” and “has” are usable words. Replacing them mechanically can
make prose strained or alter meaning. The craft question is whether the
sentence gives the reader a particular relation, not whether it passes a linter.

Source: similes, pp. 79–81.

## Change the information's job before changing its clothes

Read a longer piece for what each paragraph contributes: proposition, example,
evidence, explanation, contrast, process, terms, question, or action. If two
paragraphs do the same work, develop the second or remove it. A shorter sentence
or a new heading cannot rescue duplicated meaning.

Let the information take a useful form. A process may need steps; alternatives
may need a comparison; an experience may need a scene; an exact quotation may
need its context and attribution. A narrative can use a genuine document or
list when it changes what readers understand. Preserve the source's wording
when quoting, and make fictional documents intelligible as fiction.

Variety should correspond to a change in the reader's task. Do not force a
quotation, list, instruction, joke, or expressive fragment into every piece.
Never invent a testimonial, statistic, archival fragment, or institutional
document to make writing look more credible.

Source: theme, pp. 10–19; information textures, pp. 75–78; speech functions,
pp. 102–107; passage purpose, pp. 134–136.

## Revise where the reader loses the thread

Inspect inherited phrases for the observation they conceal. If an idiom is
clear, characteristic, or protected, it may stay. If it substitutes for missing
thought, recover the particular action or meaning. Novelty alone is no repair.

When rhythm matters, use the [spoken revision pass](rhythm-wit-warmth.md#read-aloud-test).
Check the whole artifact for repeated mechanisms as well as individual lines.
Revise the exact stumble, ambiguity, dead interval, or unnecessary explanation.
Preserve a useful pause or ordinary sentence; constant intensity gives readers
no way to distinguish what matters.

Finish with a source-to-revision check: no invented evidence, lost uncertainty,
altered promise, missing action, displaced speaker, or unfulfilled form. A craft
exercise that makes the actual artifact worse has finished teaching its lesson;
restore the stronger choice.

Source: reading aloud, pp. 49–58; clichés, pp. 66–69. Source exercises that ban
words, demand disclosure, or imitate authors are optional practice, not rules
for delivered writing.
