# Narrative craft

Use when the requested artifact needs a story: a scene, treatment, essay,
speech, personal account, or narrative within a case study or deck. Use alongside
[craft decisions](craft-decisions.md). These techniques do not change a factual
brief into permission to invent, or a practical message into a suspense story.

Page references point to the collection documented in the
[source manifest](../provenance/SOURCE-MANIFEST.md).

## Establish what changes

Before polishing a passage, identify its work. Someone may act, choose, learn,
lose an option, encounter a question, or discover that a familiar fact means
something different. The reader may gain orientation, evidence, contrast, or
needed breathing room. Change need not mean conflict or a dramatic reversal.

For a stalled narrative, separate the event sequence from its interpretation:
what happens, why the next event follows, and what that makes possible or costs.
Get that movement intelligible first. Then develop meaning through the existing
actions and details. Avoid adding a paragraph of moral explanation to compensate
for events that do not yet earn it.

Know enough about the next passage to write it. A long creative draft can
discover its eventual ending; it need not have every beat predetermined. A
bounded email, short case study, or commissioned summary still needs to finish
the stated job within its limits.

Source: next-scene purpose, pp. 102–107 and 134–136; the annotated draft on
pp. 140–160 distinguishes event movement from emotional development still to do.

## Choose the reader's question

Use the simplest order that supports the intended experience:

| Reader's question | Useful structure | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happened, and what do I need to know? | Outcome or concise overview, followed by evidence and implications. | A teasing opening that hides the actual subject. |
| How did this situation arise? | A concrete later moment, then a clearly signalled reconstruction. | Confusing presentation order with actual chronology. |
| What connects these experiences? | Several accounts held by a real shared question, setting, or concern. | Decorative framing and interchangeable examples. |
| What happens next? | A clear progression of action, consequence, and new information. | Delayed answers without an earned reason to wait. |
| Why does this keep happening? | One documented or fictional cycle whose recurrence becomes intelligible. | Invented conspiracy, motives, inevitability, or a rigged choice. |

An early overview can reassure readers that the narrator knows where the story
leads. A discovery story may need to withhold that destination. Neither is the
default for all writing. Business decisions, money, access, rights, and material
conditions stay explicit wherever a story appears around them.

A fictional conflict may become clearer by examining a challenger, a conformist,
and someone observing the consequences. Use that lens only if it illuminates
the material. Characters and real people need not fit those roles, and the
witness need not learn a tidy lesson. Preserve competing choices and unresolved
meaning when they belong to the work.

Source: framed and assembled structures, pp. 70–74; opening overview, pp. 82–84;
cycles, pp. 85–90; competing roles and the witness, pp. 91–97.

## Develop a concern through different evidence

Identify what the piece keeps returning to: a question about belonging, work,
ownership, attention, or another concern actually present in the material. Let
different moments test it. Their order and proximity should develop the reader's
understanding instead of illustrating the same slogan repeatedly.

A recurring detail earns another appearance when its consequence, ownership,
value, or implication changes. A refrain can change meaning even if its words
remain the same. Remove the return when it only announces the theme again.
Keep contrast and pauses so the pattern has room to register.

For nonfiction, select and arrange evidence without suppressing material facts
that complicate the theme. A case study's emotional arc must not turn an
unmeasured result into a success claim.

Source: theme, pp. 10–19; recurring objects, pp. 98–101 and 120–123.

## Make existing objects and promises matter

Identify details already doing useful work. An object can give someone something
to do, recall an earlier event, connect separate scenes, embody an obligation,
or enable a later consequence. Prefer developing an existing detail to adding
disposable symbols.

Track where it is, who holds it, what they know about it, and how its significance
changes. Physical continuity is part of credibility. A recurring object need
not be precious; an ordinary tool can carry both practical and emotional work.
Necessary setting and factual details may remain without a dramatic payoff.

For a resolution, look for an earlier fact, limit, action, or question that can
make it earned. Establish the necessary information before relying on it. If the
ending requires an unsupported rescue, repair the causal sequence rather than
adding a conspicuous hint. Returning to an opening image is useful only if the
return changes what it means.

In fiction, a secret or a character's lie can create pressure because people
hold different information. In nonfiction, track claims and unresolved promises
so they are supported, qualified, or answered. Never conceal a real condition
or plant a false claim to engineer a revelation.

Source: setup and payoff, pp. 41–44 and 137–139; objects, pp. 98–101 and 120–123.

## Control duration and distance

Summarise intervals whose events do not need to be experienced. Expand the
moment where a choice, discovery, gesture, or consequence matters. A simple time
transition may be stronger than making readers calculate elapsed time through
background action. Preserve exact dates and chronology in factual accounts.

A brief aside can supply relevant knowledge, reveal viewpoint, or let a moment
settle. Keep it only if returning to the action brings greater understanding or
tension. Interesting research that merely occupies time is still padding.
Attribute factual claims when attribution matters.

In a scene, dialogue and action can do different work. A gesture may identify
the speaker, create a pause, or complicate a claim. A reply may reveal a different
preoccupation instead of neatly answering the previous line. Maintain reader
orientation and use only as much attribution or physical business as needed.

Reserve deliberate non-answers for narrative subtext. Practical correspondence
should answer the recipient's question. In reported scenes, preserve quotation
boundaries and distinguish observed action from inferred motives.

Source: gesture and attribution, pp. 59–61; elapsed time, pp. 108–112;
disconnected dialogue, pp. 113–115; body language, pp. 116–119.

## Let tonal devices remain choices

Absurdity can work when recognisable details stay clear while their arrangement
becomes strange, or a trivial procedure acquires disproportionate seriousness.
Use it when the brief invites comedy or satire. Confusion and humiliation are
not substitutes for an observation.

A speaker's recurring ordinary phrase can mark a pause, reveal a habit, or let
an emotion settle. Preserve it when supported by voice evidence; do not install
it as a studio catchphrase. Let a silence remain if a line would only explain
what the reader has already understood.

End where the requested experience or obligation is complete. A narrative may
leave emotional questions open; a proposal must still resolve scope and next
steps. Do not force a twist, confession, redemption, or concluding lesson onto
the available material.

Source: absurdity, pp. 124–126; recurring phrases, pp. 127–129; worked draft,
pp. 140–160. The draft's author flags unresolved revisions and alternative
endings; its provisional choices are demonstrations, not settled rules.
