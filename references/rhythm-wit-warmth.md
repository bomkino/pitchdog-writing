# Rhythm, wit, warmth, humour, and poetry

Use this reference for expressive studio writing or when a draft feels correct
but anonymous. Information and personality develop together.

## Write in breaths, not a metronome

Vary sentence length. A longer sentence may gather context. A short one may land
the point. Then let the paragraph breathe.

Do not make every sentence a headline.

## Spoken, not sloppy

The writing should sound natural aloud:

- contractions where natural;
- concrete verbs;
- ordinary words used precisely;
- fragments when they improve timing;
- syntax a human mouth can carry.

Avoid transcript mess, filler, and fake informality.

## Paragraphs

One paragraph usually holds one movement of thought. Split a wall when scanning
or emotional pacing benefits. Do not put every sentence on its own line to
manufacture drama.

## Punctuation

Punctuation supports rhythm. It is not the voice.

- Full stops do not create wit by themselves.
- Colons are useful for clarity.
- Parentheses can hold a quiet aside.
- Dashes are occasional, not structural scaffolding.
- Exclamation marks reflect real energy; they do not rescue flat copy.

## Lists and repetition

Use lists when the reader must scan, compare, or act. Do not turn a letter,
invitation, or emotional note into a product spec merely because lists feel
organised.

Repeat to create rhythm, insistence, or emotional weight. Do not repeat because
a template has matching slots.

## Wit

Wit lets the reader see a familiar thing afresh. It can sharpen a point, make
company, puncture self-importance, or simply give pleasure. It may come from:

- a familiar phrase becoming literal;
- one word carrying two relevant jobs;
- a contradiction resolved by the next sentence;
- a technical term revealing a human benefit;
- an ordinary action becoming the service model;
- a plain sentence placed in a surprising but accurate context.

Start with an observation worth making, then find its phrasing. A professional
habit, small contradiction, disproportionate effort, or shared absurdity often
offers more than a synonym does. Let the thought develop naturally; a whole
paragraph can be lively without a punchline.

For open studio copy that feels too safe, privately try a more candid or
unexpected angle as well as the straightforward one. Choose the stronger piece,
considering recognition, timing, usefulness, and pleasure together. This is a
repair for flatness, not an options exercise for every invoice or surgical edit.

### Ownership test

- Does the line belong to this exact subject?
- Could another studio, couple, invoice, or event use it unchanged?
- Is punctuation doing more work than thought?
- Is the proposition or action easy to understand alongside the turn?
- What does the expressive version give the reader, and what would plainness lose?

If the line needs a paragraph of rescue, it has not landed.

## Humour

Our humour is dry, observational, affectionate, and comfortable taking us down
a peg. Enjoy the subject and trust the reader's intelligence. Comic timing may
be an aside, a developed observation, or the last ordinary word in a sentence.

Good targets:

- the situation;
- our own habits;
- bureaucracy;
- shared working absurdity;
- dogs behaving like actual dogs.

Bad targets:

- recipient vulnerability;
- money trouble;
- grief;
- disability;
- confusion;
- a client's mistake;
- anyone with less power in the exchange.

Keep going while the thought develops; stop when it starts explaining or
performing itself. Mild swearing can belong to a candid voice when the audience
and brief support it. Adding it cannot supply a missing observation.

## Poetry

Poetry is earned through precision, image, and rhythm. Prefer one concrete
image. Do not stack metaphors until the reader must admire the writing instead
of feeling the thing.

A wedding invitation or letter can be lyrical. An invoice should not be.

## Dog language

Dogs are characters, family, and occasional comic timing. They are not a pun
generator.

Avoid generic pet-brand language such as:

- pawsome;
- unleash your potential;
- fur-ever;
- barking good;
- fetch results.

Real dog anecdotes need real evidence. Clearly figurative play can be invented;
keep it intelligible and particular to the subject. Dogs are one part of the
studio's life, not the explanation for every service.

## Warmth under pressure

When writing about payment, delay, refusal, correction, or bad news, warmth
comes from:

- saying the difficult thing promptly;
- avoiding humiliation;
- separating fact from blame;
- making the next step possible;
- leaving room for correction or decline;
- not forcing a cheerful register.

## Read-aloud test

For consequential spoken work, read the actual wording aloud or use available
audio playback when useful. Mark the precise place a breath runs out, an actor
becomes unclear, a transition arrives too fast, or a repeated sound distracts.
Change that place, then read it again. Do not claim a spoken or listener test
when only a silent review occurred.

Allow enough time to understand a turn. A pause, attribution, or relevant action
may help in a scene; spoken scripts may need shorter clauses. Preserve authentic
rhythm rather than regularising every sentence. For screen copy, also inspect
the visual order of facts and actions; a performance cannot prove scanability.

Read for mechanism as well as sound:

- Does every paragraph use the same rhythm?
- Can the next syntactic trick be predicted?
- Is a pause carrying meaning or theatre?
- Does a line sound discovered or assembled?
- Has the writing finished one sentence earlier?
