## Actual outcome

Each heading should tell a visitor skimming the page what its section offers or proves, in words that belong to this studio and no other. Read in order, the ten should add up to the page's argument, and the line under each should resolve it with particulars.

## Costliest false success

The set looks finished. It is short, consistent, sentence case and confident, so it could pass a review as on-voice. It still fails the visitor who reads only the headings. They learn nothing about what is made, for whom, at what price or what to do next. And because every heading runs on the same machine, none of them stands out, and the page reads as assembled rather than written.

## Already alive

- **The discipline.** Sentence case, full-stop headlines, no exclamation marks, no puns, and every line can be said in one breath.
- **"Big stories. Small screens."** names a tension a visitor might recognise, the size of a story against the size of the screen it gets watched on. It holds only if the section is about that.
- **"One team. Every frame."** makes a checkable claim about who does the work. It earns its place if it is true and the section names the team.
- **"Your vision. Our craft."** points at a real arrangement, whose idea it is and who does which job. It becomes useful only when the section states the terms, such as credit and rights.

## Generic or performative

The failure is the mechanism more than any single word.

- **One skeleton, ten times.** Every heading is two fragments, each closed with a full stop. Nine are exactly two words plus two words, and "From first thought. To final cut." is three plus three. A shared voice has turned into shared syntax.
- **Every heading pivots.** Seven turn on an opposition: big and small, sharp and soft, no and just, your and our, more and less, one and every, here and everywhere. "From first thought. To final cut." is the familiar from-X-to-Y range. The remaining two repeat or escalate a single word, real and real, good and better. When every line pivots, no pivot carries weight.
- **Alliteration doing the thinking.** Stories and screens, sharp and soft, fluff and film, feeling and fuss, first and final. Half the set leans on a matching consonant where a fact should be.
- **Received text.** "Better together", "no fluff", "soft landing", "real people" and "your vision" arrive already worn. "Your vision. Our craft." could sit on any agency's site unchanged.
- **Labels in place of facts.** Real, sharp, good, better, vision and craft each ask the visitor to take the studio's word for it.
- **A full stop used as wit.** "From first thought. To final cut." splits one phrase in half. The stop adds a pause, not a thought.
- **Duplicate jobs.** "No fluff. Just film." and "More feeling. Less fuss." both promise less fuss. "One team. Every frame." and "Good work. Better together." both sell collaboration. "From first thought. To final cut." and "Made here. Seen everywhere." both describe reach. Three pairs share a job, so ten headings carry seven ideas at most.
- **The emptiest line.** "Good work. Better together." makes no claim anyone could check or disagree with.

## Material gaps

- No heading says what is actually on offer. Film is mentioned, but there is no product, format, audience, price or next step anywhere in the set, so the page can't be understood from its headings.
- Several headings make promises the page must prove or drop. "Just film" and "To final cut" define a scope. "Every frame" promises coverage. "Seen everywhere" claims reach. "Real stakes" asserts an outcome.
- "Made here" never says where here is. For a global audience it either means nothing or puts geography where the proposition should be.
- The section bodies weren't supplied, so this audit can't confirm whether anything under a heading resolves it.

## High-impact repairs

1. Before changing any wording, write down each section's one job and the one fact that proves it. Merge the sections that share a job. That alone should bring the page to seven headings or fewer.
2. Break the template. Keep at most one two-fragment heading, the one whose contrast is material, and give the rest different shapes: a plain statement, a question visitors actually ask, a figure, an instruction.
3. Put the offer's nouns into the headings: what is made, for whom, in what form, at what price, and what happens next. Wherever a label stands in for one of these, replace the label with the fact.
4. Verify or remove every scope and outcome claim: just film, final cut, every frame, seen everywhere and real stakes.
5. Cut "Good work. Better together." first. It has nothing to verify and nothing to lose.
6. Save sound play for a heading whose meaning is already exact, and use it once.
7. Test the result twice. Read the headings alone, in order, as a skimming visitor would. Then ask of each line whether another studio could use it unchanged. Any heading that fails either test goes back to step 1.
