## Actual outcome

Each heading should tell a visitor what its section is for, so that someone scrolling the page learns what the studio makes, who it is for, why it can be trusted and what to do next. Each one also needs to set up the body copy beneath it, and together they should sound like one particular studio rather than any studio.

## Costliest false success

Read as a set, these look finished: short, confident, consistent and easy to set in large type. That polish is the risk. A visitor can read all ten and still not know what is being offered, for whom or at what stage. Because every line uses the same mechanism, the headings are interchangeable. You could shuffle them between sections, or move them onto another studio's site, and nothing would visibly break. That is the clearest sign they are not yet doing section-specific work.

## Already alive

- **"Soft landing"** is the least predictable phrase in the set. It hints at something a client might actually value at the end of a job, if the section can say what the landing is.
- **"Small screens"** names a concrete condition: where work gets seen. It is the only heading that points at a real context.
- **"Your vision. Our craft."** gestures at a material arrangement: who authors the work and who executes it. That can be worth saying if the page backs it with terms about ownership and credit.
- **"first thought"** suggests the studio will take on work early. That is a useful invitation, if it is true.

## Generic or performative

- **One mechanism, ten times.** Every heading is two short fragments, each closed by a full stop, usually adjective plus noun. By the third, the reader can predict the fourth. The full stops are doing the work that an observation should do.
- **Borrowed templates.** "More feeling. Less fuss." (More X. Less Y.), "No fluff. Just film." (No X. Just Y.), "From first thought. To final cut." (From X to Y, split into two fragments by a full stop that breaks one phrase) and "Better together" (a stock slogan).
- **Contrast as a tic.** Big/small, sharp/soft, more/less and first/final all run the same antithesis. When every heading contrasts, no single contrast means anything.
- **Emphasis without evidence.** "Real people. Real stakes." repeats "real" to claim authenticity without showing any.
- **Unsupported scale.** "Seen everywhere", "Every frame", "Big stories" and "final cut" each claim reach or scope that needs proof nearby or a smaller claim.
- **Agency fog.** "Your vision. Our craft.", "One team. Every frame." and "Good work. Better together." could sit unchanged on almost any agency site.
- **Self-contradiction.** "No fluff" is itself fluff: it announces a quality rather than demonstrating it.

## Material gaps

- **No heading names the offer.** Nothing here says what is actually made or delivered.
- **Possible scope mismatch.** "Just film", "Every frame" and "final cut" describe film production. If the studio's actual work is something else, such as pitch decks, these headings promise a service it does not provide.
- **"Here" is undefined.** "Made here" asks the reader to guess a place, and a global audience has no way to.
- **No audience, proof, process or next step** appears anywhere in the set. That can be acceptable for headings only if the body copy carries all of it. The body is not included here, so check that separately.

## High-impact repairs, in order

1. **Assign each section one job before touching any wording.** Write one plain sentence per section (offer, audience, process, proof, terms, contact). Cut or merge any section whose heading cannot be tied to one of those sentences.
2. **Check every claim against what the studio actually does.** Verify or remove the film-production language, the reach claims ("Seen everywhere") and the scale claims ("Every frame", "Big stories"). Name the offer somewhere near the top.
3. **Remove the borrowed templates outright:** More/Less, No/Just, From/To and "Better together".
4. **Keep the two-fragment form for one heading at most,** and only where the contrast describes a real difference, such as the authorship split, if the page states who owns what. Give the other headings different shapes: a full sentence with a verb, a plain statement, or a literal label where visitors scan for something specific, such as pricing or contact.
5. **Resolve "here".** Either name the place and give a reason it matters to the reader, or remove the geography.
6. **Read the finished set in order, aloud.** Listen for any mechanism that repeats, then check that each body paragraph resolves its heading rather than restating it.
